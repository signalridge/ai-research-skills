# Hedging card — optional

Use this card when wording a claim, and especially when the evidence is thinner than the
sentence wants to be. The rule is one line: **claim strength never exceeds evidence strength.**

## Four rungs

| Evidence | Wording that fits |
|---|---|
| Several independent sources agree, or a meta-analysis | demonstrates, establishes, shows, confirms; consistently |
| Two or three sources, or one strong source with bounded conditions | suggests, indicates, supports the view that, is consistent with |
| One source, or a mechanism argument with no direct test | may, might, could, appears to, seems likely |
| Searched and found nothing | no work was retrieved under these queries and dates — open so far as this search goes |

## What an abstract supports

An abstract is the authors' own summary, not a result you have independently checked. It may
support an attributed, softened high-level statement about the authors' claimed direction or
conclusion (for example, "the authors report an improvement"). Abstract-only evidence does
not support a numeric result, comparison, baseline, metric, or detailed condition. Do not
write a value such as 4 points or 20% unless you have opened and recorded its named page,
table, figure, log, or section. The same rule applies when a number appears in an abstract:
attribute the broad claim, but leave the number out until its precise source location has been
read.

## Four ways this goes wrong

- **Proving.** "Proves" belongs to mathematics. Evidence supports, indicates, or fails to
  distinguish.
- **Unqualified superlatives.** "The first", "the only", "the best" are claims about the
  whole literature, and a search that did not cover the whole literature cannot support them.
  Bound them instead: "the highest score on metric M among the methods evaluated here".
- **Stacked hedges.** "It may possibly suggest that there could perhaps be" reads as evasion,
  not caution. One hedge, chosen deliberately, is enough.
- **Correlation stated as cause.** If nothing isolates the mechanism, say what was observed,
  not what produced it.

## The other failure

Hedging is not free, and over-hedging is its own inaccuracy. If a claim is well supported,
state it plainly. Do not soften an evidence-backed judgement to perform humility, and do not
manufacture a caveat to look careful — a reader cannot tell a real limitation from a decorative
one, so decorative ones cost the real ones their meaning.
